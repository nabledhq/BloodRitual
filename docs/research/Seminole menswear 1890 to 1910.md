# Seminole men's dress of 1900 predates patchwork

Around 1900, a Florida Seminole or Mikasuki-speaking man in camp wore a **loose, knee-length, pocketless cotton shirt**. It was usually a light or mid-value printed calico or stripe, with a narrow buttoned collar, a short buttoned front slit and full sleeves gathered into narrow cuffs. Younger men increasingly wore it with a sewn-in waistband and a flared skirt. It was decorated with **restrained, straight-line strips of contrasting cloth**, not the bright pieced patchwork bands that only appeared **shortly before 1920** ([Seminole Tribe of Florida](https://www.semtribe.com/culture/seminole-clothing)). Over it he wore a **stack of several bright red and yellow bandanas** knotted at the throat (up to six or eight), a **leather belt carrying pouches and a ~10-inch knife**, and, if he was older or dressed up, a **turban of wound plaid or printed wool shawls with an open crown**. His legs and feet were bare ([MacCauley 1887](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/19155/19155-h/19155-h.htm); [Skinner 1913](https://archive.org/download/jstor-659558/659558_djvu.txt)). Silver crescents, silver turban bands, plumes, woven bead sashes with long tassels, buckskin leggings and moccasins belonged to **festivals and trading-post or town visits**. Period photographs add a **dark waistcoat** that the ethnographers never mention. Measured against this evidence, the current stand-in gets the knee length, the plaid turban and the bare feet right. Its colour scheme and banding read as 1920s patchwork, it has one kerchief instead of a stack, its 1830s-style chevron sash replaces the everyday leather belt, and its short hair belongs to the 1910s or later. The likeness question is a separate issue: Seminole sources describe a taboo on realistic likenesses of people, and the Seminole Tribe of Florida's Tribal Historic Preservation Office (THPO) and the Miccosukee THPO should both be consulted before the character is finalised.

## The knee-length shirt was banded calico, not patchwork

### Two eyewitnesses bracket the period, and the cut changed between them

The period is framed by two fieldworkers. **Clay MacCauley** visited in the winter of 1880–81. He describes a shirt of "some figured or striped cotton cloth, generally of quiet colors" that "hangs from the neck to the knees, the narrow, rolling collar being closely buttoned about the neck, the narrow wristbands of the roomy sleeves buttoned about the wrists." It "opens in front for a few inches, downward from the collar, and is pocketless" ([MacCauley 1887, p. 482](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/19155/19155-h/19155-h.htm)). The belt was a separate leather or buckskin strap. **Alanson Skinner**, visiting in summer 1910, describes "a bright, varicolored, calico shirt, narrow at the waist and wrists, with the expanding skirt reaching to the knees." He adds that "the older men wear a shirt much more like the corresponding garment of civilization, which is not gathered at the waist" ([Skinner 1913, p. 65](https://archive.org/download/jstor-659558/659558_djvu.txt)). The Tribe's own clothing history, by David Blackard and Patsy West, dates the change between them: "**Around 1900**… the man's basic shirt began to incorporate a cloth waist band as a 'built in' belt" ([semtribe.com](https://www.semtribe.com/culture/seminole-clothing)).

A model dated c. 1900 can therefore defensibly show either form. The **older straight, ungathered shirt with a separate belt** suits an older man. The **waisted form**, with a fitted bodice, a cloth waistband, and a fuller skirt gathered onto it and flaring to the knee, suits a younger man. A 1904 albertype at the Library of Congress shows the waisted form clearly: a young man in a light shirt "gathered into a full skirt to just above the knee," belted ([LoC 2007678589](https://www.loc.gov/item/2007678589/)). Terminology is a trap. Sturtevant and the NativeTech pattern manual use "long shirt" for the open-front, caped, ruffled coat now called the "medicine man's coat" ([NativeTech](http://www.nativetech.org/seminole/longshirt/index.php)), while other museums use the same term for the everyday pull-over ([Florida Museum](https://www.floridamuseum.ufl.edu/100years/mans-long-shirt)). The garment to model is the **pull-over**. The Hood Museum records it as *Focsikchobee* ("big shirt") ([Hood 46.17.9956](https://hoodmuseum.dartmouth.edu/objects/46.17.9956)).

### Dated garments put the hem at the knee, about a metre below the shoulder

Several museum shirts have both dates and named owners. The Hood Museum's big shirt dated "about 1900" was collected near New River (Fort Lauderdale) around 1909 and measures **101.5 cm** in one dimension ([Hood](https://hoodmuseum.dartmouth.edu/objects/46.17.9956)). M. R. Harrington collected two shirts in 1908 that are now at the National Museum of the American Indian (NMAI). The first, a man's shirt dated 1900–1908, measures **96 × 151 × 91 cm** ([NMAI 1/7915](http://n2t.net/ark:/65665/ws6a8f7a3b9-7242-4f3c-8494-f3849d287aea)). The second, a c. 1905 shirt from Tobacco Charlie (Ingraham Tiger), measures **142 × 92 cm** ([NMAI 1/7921](http://n2t.net/ark:/65665/ws66c22f47b-9ac8-4c20-853f-bdf2a9ea02d2)). None of the records says which number is the length. The most coherent reading, which is an inference, is that the 91–102 cm figures are shoulder-to-hem lengths and the 142–151 cm figures are spans across the sleeves. On a 175 cm man that length lands the hem at or just below the knee. Shirts in a private collection dated c. 1915–18 run longer (49–57 in), and "transitional" shirts of the 1930s shrink to 30–37 in, as skirts were shortened in that decade ([Enduring Beauty catalogue, Orlando Museum of Art](https://omart.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/Enduring_Beauty_Edited_Catalogue_Complete_Final.pdf)).

### Fabric brightened between 1880 and 1910, but stayed printed and light-grounded

The cloth was trade cotton: "calico, cotton cloth, ginghams, and sometimes flannels" ([MacCauley 1887](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/19155/19155-h/19155-h.htm)). The Tribe's history notes that "calicos were most common, but stripes, solids and plaids were also used" ([semtribe.com](https://www.semtribe.com/culture/seminole-clothing)). The palette moved from MacCauley's "quiet colors" in 1880 to Skinner's "bright, varicolored" calico in 1910, and silk was reserved for gala shirts ([Skinner 1913](https://archive.org/download/jstor-659558/659558_djvu.txt)).

The photographs viewed for this brief are mostly black and white. Across 1895–1913 they show **light-ground shirts with dark bands**: the LoC group "Seminole braves" ([LoC 90710104](https://www.loc.gov/item/90710104/)), the Detroit Publishing canoe postcard no. 8207 ([NYPL](https://digitalcollections.nypl.org/items/510d47d9-a3b4-a3d9-e040-e00a18064a99)), and a 1913 Indian Office lantern slide ([Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Lantern_Slide_of_Seminole_Man,_Seated_and_Facing_Camera_-_DPLA_-_8bdc159c709da0d75f4a37762c81d04e.jpg)). Charlie Cypress, photographed in 1900, is the counter-example: he wears a dark, finely striped shirt ([Commons, FM N032022](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Charley_Cypress_Seminole_Everglades.jpg)). The only colour evidence for a banded shirt close to the period is Penn Museum's c. 1890 Fort Lauderdale shirt, "calico with crosswise stripes in many colors, with **dark blue predominating**." That description comes from a catalogue snippet; the object was not viewed ([Penn 29-47-26](https://www.penn.museum/collections/object/142223)). One tribal oral account supports the view that printed calicos, not solid fields, were the preferred ground before the First World War. Sam Tommy told the THPO that patchwork began "when fabrics preferred by Seminole crafters became unavailable during World War I," which pushed seamstresses to stitch "together patches of solid colored cloth" ([THPO 2024](https://stofthpo.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/6-June-Selling-Seminole-Success.pdf)).

### Decoration ran in straight strips at the placket, cuffs, sleeves and hem

MacCauley saw "braids and strips of cloth of various colors" worked onto garments. "Many of the shirts of the men are made pleasing to the eye. I saw **no ornamentation in curves: it was always in straight lines and angles**." On women's skirts he recorded "a border of diamond and square shaped figures" ([MacCauley 1887, p. 487](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/19155/19155-h/19155-h.htm)). The Ah-Tah-Thi-Ki Museum says early men's shirts "were primarily unadorned or showed an applique design at the **collar/chest area and the cuffs**" ([ATTK](https://www.ahtahthiki.com/its-not-a-costume/mens-shirts/)). Blackard and West add that "a decorative area usually adorned the **front placket**," and that hand-sewn appliqué "was used sparingly" ([semtribe.com](https://www.semtribe.com/culture/seminole-clothing)).

The photographs extend the picture beyond the texts. The LoC group shows **dark horizontal bands at the hem, cuffs and upper sleeves**. The 1913 lantern slide adds **vertical dark bands down the front opening** and **dark bands on the upper sleeves** ([LoC 90710104](https://www.loc.gov/item/90710104/); [Commons lantern slide](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Lantern_Slide_of_Seminole_Man,_Seated_and_Facing_Camera_-_DPLA_-_8bdc159c709da0d75f4a37762c81d04e.jpg)). One caution applies: the LoC group is dated only "between ca. 1900 and ca. 1920," so it may reflect the c. 1915–20 striping phase.

### Sewing machines came early; patchwork came two decades later

The machine-sewing chronology explains why decoration grew between 1880 and 1910 without becoming patchwork. A hand-cranked sewing machine was seen in John Willis Mikko's camp at Catfish Lake in 1880. By the 1890s machines were "a common sight in Seminole camps" ([THPO 2024](https://stofthpo.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/6-June-Selling-Seminole-Success.pdf)), and an 1892 explorer reported them in all the southeast Florida camps he visited ([semtribe.com](https://www.semtribe.com/culture/seminole-clothing)). Machines made long appliqué strips cheap, so appliqué "became common on everyday clothing" ([THPO 2024](https://stofthpo.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/6-June-Selling-Seminole-Success.pdf)).

The patchwork stages follow in order. "Just prior to 1920" seamstresses began sewing contrasting stripes *into* garments. "By 1920, both men's and women's clothing was patterned with horizontal stripes from top to bottom." **Pieced patchwork** (early "blocks or bars of alternating color or often a sawtooth design" sewn directly into the body) arrived "shortly before 1920" ([semtribe.com](https://www.semtribe.com/culture/seminole-clothing)). Big Cypress women invented it during the WWI cloth shortage ([THPO 2024](https://stofthpo.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/6-June-Selling-Seminole-Success.pdf)). Rickrack belongs to the 1940s–50s ([Seminole Tribune](https://seminoletribune.org/patchwork-exhibit-to-spotlight-high-seminole-fashion/)).

One dated object complicates this chronology. NMAI 1/7919, a man's coat collected in 1908 and dated c. 1900, is catalogued as "sewn patchwork" ([NMAI_19007](http://n2t.net/ark:/65665/ws62561ad6e-6d3c-40b1-b519-e9a080eb2807)). The cataloguer was probably using "patchwork" loosely for strip appliqué, but the object should be inspected before that is assumed. A sawtooth row is therefore documented c. 1900 only as a small cut-and-applied edging, by analogy with MacCauley's women's borders. As a pieced band, it is a c. 1917+ feature.

## Turbans, hair and neckerchiefs framed the face

### The shawl turban was wound, open-crowned and pinless

MacCauley's c. 1880 description is the construction manual. One or more small shawls, "generally woolen and copied in figure and color from the **plaid of some Scotch clan**," are folded "about **3 inches wide** and as long as the diagonal of the fabric." They are wrapped tightly round the head "**the top of the head remaining bare**," and the last end is "tucked skillfully and firmly away, **without the use of pins**." One turban he measured was "made of several shawls wound into **seven concentric rings**. It was **over 20 inches in diameter**, the shell of the cylinder being perhaps 7 inches thick and 3 in width" ([MacCauley 1887, p. 484](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/19155/19155-h/19155-h.htm)).

That 20-inch (51 cm) festival turban was large. The one dated c. 1900 turban with a named maker is **Tom Tiger's**, made 1890–1908 and collected by Harrington in 1908. It is of cotton cloth, "sewn and wrapped," and measures **30 × 12 cm** ([NMAI 1/7939](http://n2t.net/ark:/65665/ws6ed94a6ae-539d-40dd-b2aa-48725b5afcce)). The best visual reference is Arthur P. Lewis's 1895 portrait of Billy Bowlegs III. It shows a **tall turban of printed and floral-patterned shawls** wrapped over a **dark rolled band at the base** ([LoC 00649555](https://www.loc.gov/item/00649555/)). Printed shawls are therefore documented alongside plaid by 1895.

The turban's use narrowed over the period. In 1880 it was worn regularly in the southern camps and was "the customary covering" for festivals everywhere, but men usually hunted bareheaded and northern camps often used a kerchief instead. Men also carried pipes in it ([MacCauley 1887](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/19155/19155-h/19155-h.htm)). By 1910 "the **elders** also usually wear a turban made of a shawl or a series of bandanas wound together and held in place with a **broad band of beaten silver**. On special occasions, **egrets or other plumes are thrust under the band at the sides**" ([Skinner 1913, p. 65](https://archive.org/download/jstor-659558/659558_djvu.txt)). Photographs from 1901–05 show many younger men in **felt bowler-type hats**, sometimes with a feather, or in simple kerchief headbands instead ([LoC 90710104](https://www.loc.gov/item/90710104/); [LoC 2025664971](https://www.loc.gov/item/2025664971/)). Harrington collected ostrich-feather turban plumes from Billy Bowlegs III (1895–1908) and from Tobacco Charlie or Ben Frank (1900–1908; ostrich, chicken and heron, 37 cm) ([NMAI 1/8207](http://n2t.net/ark:/65665/ws60d0c70eb-59c1-45a7-9dc1-9ac1aebd5f1d); [NMAI 1/7917](http://n2t.net/ark:/65665/ws65ac52c1e-79a8-40b7-9726-e80c269206a5)).

### Hair was cropped with temple locks and twin queues, then shortened by 1910

In 1880 men cut their hair "close to the head, except a strip about an inch wide, running over the front of the scalp from temple to temple, and another strip… crossing the crown of the head to the nape." "At each temple a heavy tuft is allowed to hang to the bottom of the lobe of the ear," and the crown strip was "braided into **two ornamental queues**." A moustache was common, and a few men wore "moustache and imperial" ([MacCauley 1887, p. 486](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/19155/19155-h/19155-h.htm)). By 1910 most men "cut their hair short after the fashion of the whites, except that they are prone to **leave a lock before the ears**." Skinner adds that "it is only a short time since they have ceased to wear a double scalp-lock; indeed a few conservatives still maintain the custom" ([Skinner 1913, p. 67](https://archive.org/download/jstor-659558/659558_djvu.txt)).

The Tribe's hairstyle page gives a later date. It records Josie Billie telling Sturtevant that he and a friend were the first to adopt the white man's cut, **after 1915** ([semtribe.com](https://www.semtribe.com/culture/hairstyle)). Skinner and Josie Billie disagree on timing, but both place the older style firmly in use around 1900. Under a turban the visible elements would be the **temple locks hanging to the earlobe** and possibly **two thin braids at the nape**. No source gives queue length or says whether the braids were tucked up.

### Neckerchiefs came in stacks of red and yellow

MacCauley saw "as many as **six, even eight, handkerchiefs** tied around his throat, their **knotted ends pendant over his breast**; as a rule, they are **bright red and yellow** things, of whose possession and number he is quite proud." Money was knotted into a corner ([MacCauley 1887, p. 483](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/19155/19155-h/19155-h.htm)). Skinner in 1910 still records "a number of bandana handkerchiefs" ([Skinner 1913](https://archive.org/download/jstor-659558/659558_djvu.txt)), and the popular writer Moore-Willson describes "a half-dozen red handkerchiefs" ([Moore-Willson 1910](https://archive.org/download/seminolesofflori00will/seminolesofflori00will_djvu.txt)). The 1895 Bowlegs portrait adds **printed silk kerchiefs** over a **white neckcloth** ([LoC 00649555](https://www.loc.gov/item/00649555/)). For modelling, the inferred spec is **3–6 cotton bandanas**, mostly turkey red with one or two yellow, each folded into a band about 5–8 cm wide. They should be knotted at the front with the knots stacked under the chin and the triangular ends hanging 10–15 cm over the upper chest, so that they build visible bulk around the neck. No source specifies the knot or whether the kerchiefs sat over or under the collar.

## Belts, silver and leggings separated camp from occasion

### Everyday wear was a leather belt with pouches and a knife

MacCauley writes that "a belt of leather or buckskin usually engirdles the man's waist, and from it are suspended one or more pouches, in which powder, bullets, pocket knife, a piece of flint… are carried. From the belt hang also one or more **hunting knives, each nearly 10 inches in length**" ([MacCauley 1887, p. 483](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/19155/19155-h/19155-h.htm)). The 1904 albertype shows exactly this: a wide dark belt with a pouch at the hip ([LoC 2007678589](https://www.loc.gov/item/2007678589/)). Charlie Cypress (1900) wears a small pouch or fob on a chain at his chest ([Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Charley_Cypress_Seminole_Everglades.jpg)). Beaded fobs dated c. 1895–1905, each 10½ in long with a 2 in tab, were attached to big shirts or turbans ([Enduring Beauty](https://omart.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/09/Enduring_Beauty_Edited_Catalogue_Complete_Final.pdf)); the Hood holds one with its c. 1900 shirt ([Hood 46.17.9957](https://hoodmuseum.dartmouth.edu/objects/46.17.9957)).

### Woven sashes were festival dress, with diamonds and long tassels

In 1910 Skinner recorded "an array of **woven bead or yarn belts**" added for ceremony. Waist belts have "long, trailing tassels at the **ends and middle**," while shoulder belts have tassels "only at the ends." They are woven "in **angular figures**," whose meanings were given as "diamond-back rattlesnake, 'ground' rattlesnake, everglade terrapin, terrapin spear-point" ([Skinner 1913, pp. 65–72](https://archive.org/download/jstor-659558/659558_djvu.txt)).

Harrington's 1908 sashes give hard dimensions. Jim Gopher's sash (1890–1900), beads on finger-woven wool, measures **319 × 10.5 cm** ([NMAI 1/8203](http://n2t.net/ark:/65665/ws6f990580f-c1ea-45ed-b08e-911f9e515a6c)). Jim Osceola's loom-beaded wool sash (1895–1908) measures **255 × 7 cm** ([NMAI 1/8213](http://n2t.net/ark:/65665/ws64aa7cb9f-a347-4359-b4e0-a4d3e3566d84)). The trader Stranahan's 1897 "costume of Seminole chief" includes a woven band "5.5 ft long, ½ inch wide, **black and red with white**" ([NMNH E176087](http://n2t.net/ark:/65665/35ffee54c-bef7-42ed-bb29-e76d98d8edb0)). The LoC group photograph shows finger-woven or beaded sashes with long tassels hanging at the waist ([LoC 90710104](https://www.loc.gov/item/90710104/)).

The **chevron** finger-woven pattern is documented on Osceola's 1830s sash ([Florida Seminole Tourism](https://floridaseminoletourism.com/seminole-fashion-reflection-of-resilience/)). It is not documented for c. 1900. Beaded bandolier bags were "popular during the 1800s, with the last of them made by 1870," per the same tribal tourism source. A shoulder strap does appear on Billy Bowlegs III in 1913, but it should be treated as an heirloom or occasion piece ([Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Billy_Bowlegs_III_in_1913.jpg)).

### Silver was for festivals and trading posts

MacCauley's men wore coin-silver **crescents** (gorgets) "generally about **five inches long, an inch in width** at the widest part, and of the thickness of ordinary tin." They hung from cords at the cusps "one below another, at distances apart of perhaps **two and a half inches**." Men also wore silver wristlets tied with thongs, silver-studded belts and silver turban bands. However, "it is **not customary** for the Indian men to wear these ornaments in everyday camp life. They appear with them on a festival occasion or when they visit some trading post" ([MacCauley 1887, p. 489](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/19155/19155-h/19155-h.htm)). In 1910 Skinner records turban bands, spangles, crescents, earrings and finger-rings, hammered from coins and decorated with raised lines and bosses ([Skinner 1913, pp. 74–75](https://archive.org/download/jstor-659558/659558_djvu.txt)). A reconstruction source says gorgets were worn in graduated sets of two or three, the largest at the top with "its upper curves usually just touching at the collarbones" ([NativeTech](https://www.nativetech.org/seminole/silverwork/gorgets.html)). Men did not wear bead necklaces. Massed beads, sometimes 200 strings and 10–15 lb, were women's ornaments ([MacCauley 1887](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/19155/19155-h/19155-h.htm); [Skinner 1913](https://archive.org/download/jstor-659558/659558_djvu.txt)).

### Bare legs in camp; buckskin leggings for visits

At home a man wore "a shirt, a neckerchief, a turban, a breech cloth, and, **very rarely, moccasins**." Leggings and moccasins were added for "rare visits to the white man's settlements." Leggings were usually buckskin (MacCauley saw one red-flannel pair with blue-and-white braid), and moccasins were "yellow or dark red" buckskin laced high up the lower leg ([MacCauley 1887, pp. 483–485](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/19155/19155-h/19155-h.htm)). By 1910 "deerskin leggings dyed a **rich reddish brown**" and moccasins with "a round flap at the toe" were ceremonial only, with beaded garters below the knee ([Skinner 1913](https://archive.org/download/jstor-659558/659558_djvu.txt)). The 1904 albertype and the 1896 Tom Tiger portrait both show **side-fringed leggings** with moccasins ([LoC 2007678589](https://www.loc.gov/item/2007678589/); [Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Captain_Tom_Tiger.jpg)). The LoC group and the 1901 Detroit postcard show men **barefoot** ([LoC 90710104](https://www.loc.gov/item/90710104/); [LoC 2025664971](https://www.loc.gov/item/2025664971/)). Neither ethnographer mentions trousers as camp wear in this period.

### The waistcoat is the photographs' surprise

Neither MacCauley nor Skinner mentions a vest. Yet a **dark waistcoat over the shirt** appears in most posed photographs viewed for this brief: the 1895 Bowlegs portrait, the 1901 Detroit postcard, the 1904 albertype, the LoC group, the Detroit canoe negatives and Bowlegs in 1913 ([LoC 00649555](https://www.loc.gov/item/00649555/); [LoC 2025664971](https://www.loc.gov/item/2025664971/); [LoC 2007678589](https://www.loc.gov/item/2007678589/); [LoC 2016814260](https://www.loc.gov/item/2016814260/)). It is absent from the field images of Charlie Cypress (1900) and the 1913 lantern slide. The likeliest reading, which is an inference, is that the vest was a town, trading-post and photograph layer. That makes it a strong optional variant rather than the camp default.

## The stand-in reads as 1920s patchwork worn with an 1830s sash

The current stand-in mixes accurate elements with later patchwork-era and earlier pre-removal signals. The table separates what to keep from what to change, in order of visual impact.

| Stand-in element | c. 1900 evidence | Correction |

## Consult the Seminole and Miccosukee THPOs before finalising the face

The most serious cultural issue is not the clothing; it is the face. A Seminole Tribe tourism article on dolls states that "a realistic doll would bring harm to the person it looked like, as well as to the person who made it" ([Florida Seminole Tourism](https://floridaseminoletourism.com/?p=6394)). Museum commentary records that doll makers "continued to respect cultural taboos about representation by limiting facial features" ([UVM Library](https://libraryexhibits.uvm.edu/omeka/exhibits/show/humanform/item/595)). No tribal source addresses digital or 3D characters directly, so the question must be asked. Until it is answered, the face should be an original composite, never built from a photograph or scan of a real person, and the character should not be named after a historical figure such as Tom Tiger or Billy Bowlegs.

Other topics should stay out of the game entirely. "Most details concerning the Seminole Indian medicine culture… are not discussed outside the Tribe" ([semtribe.com](https://www.semtribe.com/culture/medicine)). The Green Corn Dance is "a special spiritual event held at undisclosed South Florida locations" that few non-Indians have witnessed ([semtribe.com](https://www.semtribe.com/culture/green-corn-dance)). This is also why the festival-dress variant (plumes, silver, ceremonial sashes and leggings) belongs only in public contexts the Tribe approves. Clans can be referenced only at the level the Tribe publishes: eight extant matrilineal clans ([semtribe.com](https://www.semtribe.com/culture/clans)).

Language follows geography. After the wars, Mikasuki-speaking people settled the southern swamps and Muscogee (Creek)-speaking people the central lands ([semtribe.com](https://www.semtribe.com/history/a-changing-florida)). A Big Cypress or Everglades camp therefore implies Mikasuki speakers and the shared ancestry of today's Seminole Tribe of Florida and Miccosukee Tribe. A camp north of Lake Okeechobee or at Cow Creek implies Creek speakers. No dialogue should ship without a fluent, community-approved speaker.

None of the three tribes publishes game or media guidelines, and there is no known Seminole- or Miccosukee-consulted game to follow. The working precedents are elsewhere. On *Never Alone*, Alaska Native elders reviewed concept art and playable demos on repeated trips ([Peninsula Clarion](https://peninsulaclarion.com/news/native-american-studio-releases-inupiat-game)). On *Civilization VII*, Firaxis approached Shawnee tribal leadership directly and funded language revitalisation ([AP via News4Jax](https://www.news4jax.com/business/2024/10/04/civilization-7-makers-work-with-shawnee-to-bring-sincere-representation-of-the-tribe-to-the-game/)). FSU's Osceola regalia shows that the Seminole Tribe of Florida does approve portrayals, but only within an ongoing relationship in which the Tribe "designed and approved" the regalia ([ICT News](https://ictnews.org/news/chief-osceola-and-renegade-have-dazzled-florida-state-fans-with-tribes-approval-for-nearly-50-years/)).

The process proposed here is an inference from those precedents, not tribal policy. Pay consultants professionally. Set review gates at the character brief, the model, dialogue and names, and a playable build. Agree in writing that the community can veto sensitive content. Until review, label the stand-in in-build as "Placeholder art — not reviewed or endorsed by the Seminole Tribe of Florida or the Miccosukee Tribe of Indians of Florida," and keep it out of marketing.

## Conclusion

The stand-in's errors share a cause: it borrows the most recognisable Seminole visual signatures, which come from other decades. Bright pieced bands are a 1920s invention, the chevron finger-woven sash is an 1830s Osceola-era form, and short hair is a post-1910 change. The documented c. 1900 man is quieter and more layered. His colour sits at the throat (the red and yellow kerchief stack) and on his head (the plaid turban), not in the shirt body. The most useful single fix is to rebalance the colour that way: a light printed shirt with thin dark straight strips, and a mass of red and yellow at the neck. That one change moves the silhouette from "patchwork icon" to "1900 camp."

Two open questions should drive the next round. Band count and width on c. 1900 shirts are inferred, not measured. Full-resolution views of LoC 90710104 and NYPL 8207, and an Ah-Tah-Thi-Ki appointment to see Harrington-era shirts, would settle them. More consequential is whether a realistic face is acceptable at all under the likeness taboo. Only the Seminole Tribe of Florida and the Miccosukee Tribe can answer that, and their answer could change the character's design more than any clothing correction.
